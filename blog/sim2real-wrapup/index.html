World Models for Sim2Real Style Transfer

November 9, 2026 · Jiayu Zhou

We ask whether a video world model fine-tuned on a few minutes of real-world video can serve as the renderer of a physics simulator, closing the visual domain gap for policy evaluation and policy training. On a tight-tolerance gear insertion task, it can.

Summary

Fine-tuned on ten minutes of real-world video, the world model renders the simulator realistically enough that simulated evaluation ranks policies as real-world evaluation does, and that policies trained on its output transfer better than policies trained on native or manually calibrated renderings.

Setting. The physics simulator remains the source of states and actions.
A video world model (Cosmos3-Nano), fine-tuned on real-world video from our own cameras, replaces the rendered observation of every frame.
We call this augmented simulation (augsim).
The task is tight-tolerance gear insertion with a Franka FR3, three cameras, and ACT policies trained by behavior cloning on scripted demonstrations.
Every policy is evaluated on the same 16 scenes in raw sim, in augsim, and in the real world.

Evaluation fidelity. Augsim ranks policies in the same order as real-world evaluation and identifies the policy that fails in the real world.
Raw sim ranks them in reverse.
Neither predicts the absolute success rate.

Training transfer. With identical demonstrations and actions, the policy trained on style-transferred observations achieves the highest real-world success, and the policy trained in the manually calibrated renderer never grasps the gear.

1. Problem: the visual domain gap in simulation-based policy evaluation

A simulator with correct physics can still mislead policy evaluation, because a visuomotor policy observes pixels, not physics.

Our first policy seated the gear in all simulated scenes and in half of the real ones.
When three policies were compared, the simulator ranked them in the reverse of the real-world order and would have selected the policy that never grasped a gear.

Manual appearance calibration did not close the gap.
We matched the simulator's table, lighting and camera response to the real frames and applied domain randomization around them.
The resulting policy was the worst in the real world: the real table has wood grain, and no randomized parameter produced texture.

A video world model conditioned on edges and depth renders texture, keeps it temporally consistent, and preserves geometry.
If such a model can be adapted to one scene from a few minutes of real-world video, the simulator can be rendered in that scene's appearance and become a realistic space for training and evaluation.
The rest of this post tests that claim.

We chose a sub-millimeter insertion task deliberately.
Every error source is exposed, so the contribution of appearance can be separated from the contribution of geometry and dynamics.

2. Method: augmented simulation with a fine-tuned video world model

The simulator keeps the state; the world model replaces only the rendered observation.

Terminology. Raw sim is the simulator's native rendering.
Augsim is the simulator rendered through the adapted world model.
Ref_sim is the manually calibrated renderer with domain randomization.

ComponentChoice
SimulatorMuJoCo with a port of the real robot's Polymetis controller, signed-distance-field contacts for gear and shaft, cameras from the current real-world calibration

Design alternatives rejected
  • Control signals estimated from real video instead of rendered by the simulator: lower fidelity and hallucinated grippers.
  • A segmentation control channel: gripper and gear merged.
  • Training windows sampled from episode centers with a defective wrist mask: the gear vanished from style-transferred frames.
    Sampling windows across whole episodes fixed it.

3. Scene adaptation of the video world model

Ten minutes of real-world video are sufficient to adapt the world model to this scene.
It renders our table, lighting and gripper, and holds them steady across frames.

Scene adaptation from a few minutes of video works.
The style-transferred stream is clearly closer to the real cameras, not identical to them, and autoregressive rendering keeps it temporally consistent.
Whether that is close enough is the subject of sections 4 and 5.

3.1 Inference latency and optimization

For augmented simulation to be usable, rendering must be cheap enough for every frame of every demonstration and every decision of a closed-loop evaluation.
Our first renderer, Cosmos-Transfer2.5 at 720 pixels and 35 denoising steps, took 7 s per decision frame.

Four changes, in order of effect:

  • Fewer denoising steps. From 35 to 10, the count used for the training data.
    The largest single gain; it also made closed-loop rendering consistent with training.
  • A smaller model at the policy's resolution. Cosmos3-Nano at 256 pixels instead of Cosmos-Transfer2.5 at 720 pixels.
  • Rendering streams instead of frames. An autoregressive window amortizes the fixed cost of a request: 0.05 s per frame against 1.8 s for a single decision frame.
  • Caching across denoising steps. cache_dit removes 14 percent of model time at 34 dB PSNR against the uncached output.
    Flash attention and torch.compile were already enabled in the serving stack.
    The Cosmos3 pipeline does not support request batching, so throughput scales only with one server per GPU.

We have not profiled inside the model.
The denoising step count is the measured lever, and per-step cost dominates.
Sharing a GPU between the rendering server and a second evaluation process slowed each request from 1.9 s to about 5 s, so each server requires a dedicated GPU.

4. Evaluation fidelity: does augmented simulation predict real-world performance?

Augsim is a reliable instrument for comparing policies: it ranks them as real-world evaluation does and identifies the policy that fails.
It does not predict absolute success rates.

Five policies of different quality were evaluated on the same 16 scenes under three conditions.
They differ in training observations and data size, which makes them a test of ranking.

PolicyTraining observationsDemonstrations
sim1024raw sim1024
sim128raw sim128
augsim128augsim (Cosmos3-Nano)128
ref_sim1024ref_sim with domain randomization1024
mix512raw sim512, half of them recovery demonstrations
  • Ranking. Real-world evaluation ordered the three newer policies augsim128, sim128, ref_sim1024.
    Augsim reproduced that order.
    Raw sim reversed it and ranked first the policy that never grasped a gear in the real world.
  • Per-scene agreement. Augsim agreed with the real outcome on more scenes than raw sim for every policy except augsim128, whose training observations are themselves Cosmos3-Nano renderings.
  • Absolute rates. Both simulators miss them, in both directions.
  • Failure modes. Real rollouts fail before contact: the gripper closes beside the gear.
    Raw sim fails late, at the shaft.
    Augsim fails in between.
    Neither reproduces the real grasp misses, which arise from camera calibration drift and arm settling error (section 6).

Use augsim to rank candidate policies and to detect a policy that will fail in the real world.
Do not read its success rate as a prediction of the real one.

Camera calibration used for each simulated evaluation

Every simulated number is the one measured before the corresponding real-world test, with the camera calibration that policy was trained and evaluated with.
For sim1024 that is its own calibration (round 27b); its augsim evaluation is the exception, run after the real-world test with the recalibrated cameras (round 29).
The three newer policies were trained and evaluated under round 29 throughout. mix512 is an earlier policy (October 13): its sim and augsim evaluations warp the cameras to that day's real poses, and its augsim used Cosmos-Transfer2.5, the renderer before Cosmos3-Nano.

5. Training transfer: policies trained on style-transferred observations

Augsim also works as a training space: the same demonstrations rendered through the world model yield a policy that transfers better than raw or manually calibrated rendering.

  • sim128, the control, trained on raw sim observations.
  • augsim128, the treatment, trained on the same frames rendered autoregressively by Cosmos3-Nano.
  • ref_sim1024, the manual-calibration alternative, trained on 1024 demonstrations rendered in ref_sim with domain randomization and image augmentation.

All three share the training recipe and the real-world test: 16 scenes, the same cameras and placements, within two days of each other.

Rendering the training observations through the world model moved real-world success in the right direction.
The gain over raw sim is within the noise of 16 scenes; the zero of ref_sim is not, and augsim predicted it before the real-world test.

6. Mechanism: how appearance transfer changes policy outputs

From the style-transferred frame the policy predicts almost the action chunk it predicts from the real frame, because style transfer changes the image features the policy reads, not the regions it attends to.

Real-world rollouts store the observations the policy consumed and the joint state at every decision.
Before the first grasp the scene is still the seed's layout, so we place the simulated arm at the measured state, render, apply style transfer, and compare the action chunks the policy predicts from the raw, style-transferred and real observations: 128 matched states over four policies.

Spatial attention versus feature content. The policy attends to the same regions in all three observations.
What differs is the feature content at those regions, and the table, background and gripper carry the difference, not the gears.

Residual error not attributable to appearance. Replaying the eight successful real-world action sequences open-loop in the simulator produced eight grasps and two seatings.
The simulated tool center drifts by millimeters from the real one, and a sub-millimeter insertion does not tolerate that.

Style transfer changes the texture the policy reads in the table and background, mostly at the first decision, and that brings its predicted chunk to the real-observation chunk.
The remaining failures, grasps beside the gear and stalled insertions, are due to calibration and dynamics.

7. Conclusions, limitations and future work

On this task, a world model fine-tuned on ten minutes of real-world video turns the simulator into a realistic space for policy evaluation and training.
Geometry and dynamics errors remain.

Conclusions, for this task and setup.

  • Evaluation. Augsim ranks policies in the real-world order and identifies the policy that will fail.
    It does not estimate success rates.
  • Training. Policies trained on style-transferred demonstrations transferred better than policies trained on raw or manually calibrated renderings: a direction measured on 16 scenes, not an effect size.
  • Mechanism. Style transfer changes the image features the policy reads, not the regions it attends to.
  • Residual gap. The remaining real-world failures are not visual.
    A camera drift of a fraction of a degree displaces the grasp by a centimeter, and the arm settles millimeters from its commanded pose.

Limitations.

  • 16 scenes per condition, one task, one setup, one recording session for the model's real data.
  • Per-frame style transfer in the closed loop flickers, and 2 s per frame precludes online use.
  • The adapted model renders the same image for every prompt.
    The base model's prompt following, which RoboRender uses to randomize textures and distractors, is lost.
  • Each camera is rendered independently; multi-view consistency is not enforced.
  • The model is faithful under camera perturbations of up to about 20 degrees and 20 centimeters while the view stays on the table.
    When the view includes the robot pedestal or the floor, it hallucinates a plausible laboratory.

Future work.

  • A state-based teacher policy that solves the task in simulation, distilled into a visuomotor student on style-transferred observations.
  • Renderer selection by learning outcome rather than visual fidelity: fine-tune clones of one policy briefly on the same states rendered by each candidate and select the renderer whose action error on real observations falls fastest.
  • The training comparison repeated at 512 to 1024 demonstrations with checkpoint selection in augsim.
  • A model of the arm's settling error in the simulator.

F. Comparison with RoboRender

RoboRender (Huang, Ai, Li Fei-Fei, Jiajun Wu and others, October 2026) asks whether a video model can make simulated data good enough to train real-world policies at scale, and answers yes for visually driven tasks.
This project asks how little real data is sufficient, whether a style-transferred simulator can evaluate a policy, and what remains when the task is contact-rich.
