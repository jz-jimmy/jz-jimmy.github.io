World Models for Sim2Real Style Transfer

November 9, 2026 · Jiayu Zhou

Can a video world model fine-tuned on a few minutes of real-world video serve as the renderer of a physics simulator and bridge the rendering gap, making simulation realistic enough for policy evaluation and training? On tight-tolerance gear insertion, yes.

Summary

Ten minutes of real-world video are enough.
Fine-tuned on them, a video world model renders simulation realistically enough that simulated evaluation ranks policies as real-world evaluation does, and policies trained on its renderings transfer better than policies trained on native or manually calibrated renderings.

Setting. Physics stays in the simulator.
A video world model (Cosmos3-Nano), fine-tuned on real-world video from our cameras, renders what the policy sees.
We call this augmented simulation, or augsim.
Our task is tight-tolerance gear insertion with a Franka FR3, three cameras, and ACT policies trained by behavior cloning on scripted demonstrations.
Every policy runs on 16 fixed scenes in raw sim, in augsim, and in the real world.

As an evaluation space. Augsim ranks policies as real-world evaluation does and identifies which one fails.
Raw sim ranks them in reverse.
Neither predicts absolute success rate.

As a training space. Same demonstrations, same actions, only pixels changed: training on style-transferred observations gives the best real-world success, and training in a manually calibrated renderer gives zero grasps.

1. Problem: rendering gap and dynamics gap

The sim-to-real gap has two components, a rendering gap and a dynamics gap.
We hold the dynamics gap as small as we can and ask whether a fine-tuned video world model can bridge the rendering gap, so that simulation becomes realistic enough for policy training and evaluation.

Rendering gap. Our first policy seated the gear in every simulated scene and in half of real ones.
When we compared three policies, raw sim ranked them in reverse of real-world order and would have selected a policy that never grasps a gear.

Manual appearance calibration did not help.
We matched table, lighting and camera response to real frames and randomized around them.
The resulting policy was our worst in the real world.
Real tables have wood grain; no randomized parameter produces texture.

A video world model conditioned on edges and depth does produce texture, keeps it consistent over time, and leaves geometry alone.
If a few minutes of real-world video adapt such a model to one scene, simulation inherits that scene's appearance.
This post tests whether that bridges the rendering gap.

Dynamics gap, held fixed. One simulator serves every condition: a port of the real robot's controller, signed-distance-field contacts, cameras from current calibration (section 2, appendix B).
What remains of the dynamics gap appears in section 6 as residual error.

We chose sub-millimeter insertion deliberately.
Both gaps are exposed, so the contribution of rendering can be separated from dynamics.

2. Method: augmented simulation with a fine-tuned video world model

Simulation keeps state; the world model replaces only rendered observations.

Terminology. Raw sim: the simulator's native rendering.
Augsim: simulation rendered through the adapted world model.
Ref_sim: a manually calibrated renderer with domain randomization.

ComponentChoice
SimulatorMuJoCo with a port of the real robot's Polymetis controller, signed-distance-field contacts for gear and shaft, cameras from current real-world calibration
PolicyACT with three 224-pixel views and proprioception, predicting chunks of 100 joint targets at 30 Hz; behavior cloning on scripted full-insertion demonstrations
Real-world data20 scripted episodes recorded by three cameras, about ten minutes in total, disjoint from policy data

InferenceDemonstration streams rendered autoregressively at 0.06 s per frame; in closed-loop evaluation each decision frame is rendered on its own in about 2 s while simulation waits

Design alternatives rejected
  • Control signals estimated from real video instead of rendered by the simulator: lower fidelity and hallucinated grippers.
  • A segmentation control channel: gripper and gear merged.
  • Training windows sampled from episode centers with a defective wrist mask: gears vanished from style-transferred frames.
    Sampling windows across whole episodes fixed it.

3. Scene adaptation of the video world model

Ten minutes of real-world video adapt the world model to our scene.
It renders our table, lighting and gripper, and holds them steady across frames.

Adaptation from a few minutes of video works.
Style-transferred frames are clearly closer to real cameras, not identical to them, and autoregressive rendering keeps them temporally consistent.
Whether close enough is a question for sections 4 and 5.

3.1 Inference latency and optimization

Augmented simulation is only useful if rendering is cheap: every frame of every demonstration, every decision of a closed-loop evaluation.
Our first renderer, Cosmos-Transfer2.5 at 720 pixels and 35 denoising steps, needed 7 s per decision frame.

Four changes brought it down, in order of effect.
Fewer denoising steps, 35 to 10, matching the training data; the largest single gain, and it made closed-loop rendering consistent with training.
A smaller model at policy resolution, Cosmos3-Nano at 256 pixels.
Streams instead of frames: an autoregressive window amortizes per-request overhead, 0.05 s per frame against 1.8 s for a single decision frame.
Caching across denoising steps: cache_dit removes 14 percent of model time at 34 dB PSNR against uncached output.
Flash attention and torch.compile were already on.
Cosmos3 does not support request batching, so throughput scales only by one server per GPU.

We have not profiled inside the model; step count is the measured lever and per-step cost dominates.
One practical note: a rendering server sharing its GPU with a second evaluation process slowed from 1.9 s to about 5 s per request.
One server, one GPU.

4. Evaluation fidelity: does augmented simulation predict real-world performance?

Augsim is a reliable instrument for comparing policies.
It ranks them as real-world evaluation does and flags the one that fails.
It does not predict absolute success rates.

We evaluated five policies of different quality on the same 16 scenes under three conditions.
They differ in training observations and data size, which is what makes them a ranking test.

PolicyTraining observationsDemonstrations
sim1024raw sim1024
sim128raw sim128
augsim128augsim (Cosmos3-Nano)128
ref_sim1024ref_sim with domain randomization1024
mix512raw sim512, half of them recovery demonstrations

Ranking. Real-world evaluation ordered our three newer policies augsim128, sim128, ref_sim1024.
Augsim reproduced that order.
Raw sim reversed it and ranked first a policy that never grasped a gear in the real world.

Per-scene agreement. Augsim agreed with real outcomes on more scenes than raw sim for every policy except augsim128, whose training observations are themselves Cosmos3-Nano renderings.

Failure modes. Real rollouts fail before contact: fingers close beside the gear.
Raw sim fails late, at the shaft.
Augsim fails in between.
Neither reproduces real grasp misses, which come from camera calibration drift and arm settling (section 6).
Absolute rates are off in both simulators, in both directions.

Use augsim to rank candidates and to catch a policy that will fail in the real world.
Do not read its success rate as a prediction.

Camera calibration used for each simulated evaluation

Every simulated number was measured before its real-world test, with the camera calibration that policy was trained and evaluated with.
For sim1024 that is its own calibration (round 27b); its augsim evaluation is the exception, run after the real-world test with recalibrated cameras (round 29).
Our three newer policies were trained and evaluated under round 29 throughout. mix512 is an earlier policy (October 13): its sim and augsim evaluations warp cameras to that day's real poses, and its augsim used Cosmos-Transfer2.5, our renderer before Cosmos3-Nano.

5. Training transfer: policies trained on style-transferred observations

Augsim also works as a training space.
Same demonstrations, rendered through the world model, give a policy that transfers better than raw or manually calibrated rendering.

Three policies share one recipe and one real-world test: 16 scenes, same cameras and placements, within two days.
sim128, the control, trained on raw sim.
augsim128, the treatment, trained on the same frames rendered autoregressively by Cosmos3-Nano.
ref_sim1024, the manual-calibration alternative, trained on 1024 demonstrations rendered in ref_sim with domain randomization and image augmentation.

Realistic training observations moved real-world success in the right direction.
The gain over raw sim is within noise at 16 scenes; ref_sim's zero is not, and augsim predicted it before the real-world test.

6. Mechanism: how appearance transfer changes policy outputs

From a style-transferred frame, the policy predicts almost the same action chunk as from the real frame.
Style transfer changes what the policy reads, not where it looks.

Real-world rollouts store every observation the policy consumed and the joint state at every decision.
Before the first grasp, the scene is still the seed's layout, so we place the simulated arm at the measured state, render, apply style transfer, and compare action chunks predicted from raw, style-transferred and real observations: 128 matched states over four policies.

Where versus what. Attention lands on the same regions in all three observations.
Feature content at those regions differs, and table, background and gripper carry the difference, not gears.

What rendering cannot fix. Replaying eight successful real-world action sequences open-loop in simulation gave eight grasps and two seatings.
Simulated tool center drifts by millimeters from the real one, and sub-millimeter insertion does not tolerate millimeters.

Style transfer changes texture in table and background, mostly at the first decision, and that brings predicted actions to where real observations put them.
Remaining failures, grasps beside the gear and stalled insertions, are calibration and dynamics.

7. Conclusions, limitations and future work

On this task, a world model fine-tuned on ten minutes of real-world video turns simulation into a realistic space for policy evaluation and training.
Geometry and dynamics errors remain.

Conclusions, for this task and setup.

  • Evaluation. Augsim ranks policies in real-world order and identifies which one will fail.
    It does not estimate success rates.
  • Training. Policies trained on style-transferred demonstrations transferred better than policies trained on raw or manually calibrated renderings: a direction measured on 16 scenes, not an effect size.
  • Mechanism. Style transfer changes image features the policy reads, not regions it attends to.
  • Residual gap. Remaining real-world failures are not visual.
    Camera drift of a fraction of a degree displaces a grasp by a centimeter, and the arm settles millimeters from its commanded pose.

Limitations.

  • 16 scenes per condition, one task, one setup, one recording session for the model's real data.
  • Per-frame style transfer in the closed loop flickers, and 2 s per frame precludes online use.
  • The adapted model renders the same image for every prompt.
    Prompt following, which RoboRender uses to randomize textures and distractors, is lost.
  • Each camera is rendered independently; multi-view consistency is not enforced.
  • Rendering stays faithful under camera perturbations up to about 20 degrees and 20 centimeters while the view stays on the table.
    Once the view includes robot pedestal or floor, the model hallucinates a plausible laboratory.

Future work.

  • A state-based teacher policy that solves the task in simulation, distilled into a visuomotor student on style-transferred observations.
  • Renderer selection by learning outcome rather than visual fidelity: fine-tune clones of one policy briefly on the same states rendered by each candidate and keep the renderer whose action error on real observations falls fastest.
  • The training comparison repeated at 512 to 1024 demonstrations with checkpoint selection in augsim.
  • A model of arm settling error in the simulator.

F. Comparison with RoboRender

RoboRender (Huang, Ai, Li Fei-Fei, Jiajun Wu and others, October 2026) asks whether a video model can make simulated data good enough to train real-world policies at scale, and answers yes for visually driven tasks.
This project asks how little real data is sufficient, whether a style-transferred simulator can evaluate a policy, and what remains when the task is contact-rich.
