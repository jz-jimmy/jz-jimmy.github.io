World Models for Sim2Real Style Transfer

November 9, 2026 · Jiayu Zhou

Can ten minutes of real video make a simulator look real enough to evaluate and train robot policies? A controlled study on one hard task.

Summary

Ten minutes of real footage turn a physics simulator into one whose verdicts match the real-world results: it ranks policies in the real order, catches the policy that will fail, and trains policies that transfer better.

We fine-tuned a video world model (Cosmos3-Nano) on about ten minutes of real footage and use it as the renderer: the simulator keeps advancing the physics, the model repaints every frame to look like our cameras. We call the result augmented simulation, or augsim. The task is tight-tolerance gear insertion with a Franka FR3, three cameras and ACT policies trained on scripted demonstrations. Every policy runs on the same 16 scenes in raw sim, in augsim and on the real robot.

Evaluate in augsim, not in raw sim. Augsim ranks policies in the same order as the real-world evaluation; raw sim ranked them backwards. Neither predicts the success rate itself.

Train on repainted frames. Same demonstrations, same actions, only the pixels changed: the policy trained on repainted frames did best on the robot, and the one trained in a simulator matched to the cameras by hand never grasped a gear.

1. Why improve simulated visual observations

A simulator can be right about the physics and still mislead you, because the policy never sees physics. It sees pixels.

Our first policy seated the gear in every simulated scene and in half of the real ones. Worse, when three policies had to be compared, the simulator ordered them backwards and would have shipped the one that never grasped a gear in the real world.

Hand-built realism did not help. We matched the simulator's table, light and camera response to the real frames, randomized around them, and got our worst real policy: the real table has wood grain, and nothing we randomized produced texture. A video model paints texture, keeps it steady across frames and, conditioned on edges and depth, leaves the geometry alone. The question was whether a few minutes of one scene are enough.

We chose a sub-millimeter insertion on purpose. Every error source shows up, so we can separate what appearance fixes from what it cannot.

2. Building augmented simulation

The simulator keeps the state; the world model only replaces what the cameras would see.

PieceChoice
SimulatorMuJoCo with a port of the real robot's Polymetis controller, signed-distance-field contacts for the gear and shaft, cameras from the current real calibration
PolicyACT: three 224-pixel views plus proprioception, chunks of 100 joint targets at 30 Hz, behavior cloning on scripted full-insertion demonstrations
Real footage20 scripted episodes on the real robot, about ten minutes across three cameras, separate from the policy data
RendererCosmos3-Nano (8 B parameters) post-trained at 256 pixels, conditioned on simulator-rendered edges and inverse depth with a fixed caption
GenerationTraining streams repainted autoregressively at 0.06 s per frame; in the closed loop each decision frame is repainted on its own in about 2 s while the simulator waits
Protocol16 seeds fix the gear placement; the same seeds run in raw sim, in augsim and on the robot; success is the gear fully seated
What we tried and dropped
  • Controls estimated from real video instead of rendered: lower fidelity and invented grippers.
  • A segmentation channel: merged gripper and gear.
  • Training windows from episode centers with a bad wrist mask: the gear vanished from repainted frames. Sampling windows across whole episodes fixed it.

3. Why video models and scene adaptation

The base model paints a plausible lab. Adaptation makes it paint this lab: its table, its light, its gripper.

Ten minutes move the model to this scene: clearly closer to the cameras, clearly not the same. Repainting the stream autoregressively rather than frame by frame is what removes the flicker.

4. Predicting real-world performance

Augsim predicts which policy will do better on the robot, not how often it will succeed.

Five policies of different quality, the same 16 scenes, three settings. They differ in training images and data size, which is what makes them a test of ranking.

PolicyTraining imagesDemonstrations
sim1024raw sim1024
sim128raw sim128
augsim128Nano-repainted sim128
ref_sim1024hand-matched sim with randomization1024
mix512raw sim512, half of them recovery demonstrations
  • Ranking. The real-world evaluation ordered the three newer policies augsim128, sim128, ref_sim1024. Augsim gave the same order. Raw sim gave the reverse and put the policy that never grasped a real gear first.
  • Per scene. Augsim agreed with the real outcome on more scenes than raw sim for every policy but augsim128, whose training images are themselves Nano frames.
  • Rates. Both simulators miss them, in both directions.
  • Failure types. Real rollouts fail before contact: the fingers close beside the gear. Raw sim fails late, at the shaft. Augsim fails in between. Neither reproduces the real grasp misses, which come from calibration drift and arm settling (section 6).

Use augsim to rank candidates and to catch a policy that will fail on the robot. Do not read its success rate as the real one.

Camera calibration behind each simulated result

Every simulated number is the one measured before its real test, with the cameras that policy was trained and evaluated with. For sim1024 that is its own calibration (round 27b); its augsim run is the exception, made after the real test with the recalibrated cameras (round 29). The three newer policies were trained and evaluated under round 29 throughout. mix512 is an earlier policy (October 13): its sim and augsim runs warp the cameras to that day's real poses, and its augsim used Cosmos-Transfer2.5, the renderer before Nano.

5. Training policies that transfer

Same 128 demonstrations, same states and actions, only the training images differ: the policy trained on repainted frames did best on the robot.

  • sim128, the control, trained on the raw renders.
  • augsim128, the treatment, trained on the same frames repainted autoregressively by Nano.
  • ref_sim1024, the hand-matched alternative, trained on 1024 demonstrations rendered in the matched, randomized sim with image augmentation.

All three use the same recipe and the same real test: 16 scenes, same cameras, same placements, within two days of each other.

Closing the appearance gap in the training data moved real success in the right direction. The gain over raw frames is within the noise of 16 scenes; the zero of the hand-matched sim is not, and augsim flagged it before the real test.

6. How visual transfer changes policy decisions

Hold the policy and the state fixed and change only the image: from the repainted frame the policy plans almost what it plans from the real frame.

Real rollouts store the images the policy consumed and the joint state at every decision. Before the first grasp the scene is still the seed's layout, so we put the simulated arm at the measured state, render, repaint, and compare the three plans the policy makes from raw sim, augsim and the real image: 128 matched states over four policies.

Where in the image, and what? Not where we expected. The policy looks at the same places in all three frames. What differs is what it reads there, and the table, background and gripper carry the difference, not the gears.

The part no renderer can fix. Replaying the real robot's eight successful action sequences open-loop in the simulator grasped eight times and seated twice. The simulated tool center drifts millimeters from the real one, and a sub-millimeter insertion does not forgive that.

Repainting changes the texture the policy reads in the table and background, mostly at the first decision, and that brings its plan to the real-image plan. The failures that remain, grasps beside the gear and insertions that stall, are calibration and dynamics.

7. Findings and next steps

Appearance was the part of the gap that ten minutes of video could close. What remains is geometry and dynamics.

Findings, for this task and setup.

  • Augsim ranks policies in the real-world order and catches the one that will fail on the robot. It does not estimate success rates.
  • Training on repainted demonstrations transferred better than training on raw or hand-matched ones: a direction measured on 16 scenes, not an effect size.
  • The mechanism is texture, not attention.
  • The remaining real failures are not visual. A camera drift of a fraction of a degree moves the grasp by a centimeter, and the arm settles millimeters from its command.

Limitations.

  • 16 scenes per setting, one task, one setup, one recording session for the model's real data.
  • Per-frame repainting in the closed loop flickers, and 2 s per frame rules out online use.
  • The adapted model paints the same image for every prompt. The base model's prompt following, the lever RoboRender uses to randomize textures and distractors, is lost.
  • Each camera is repainted on its own; multiview consistency is not enforced.
  • The renderer is faithful under camera offsets of up to about 20 degrees and 20 centimeters as long as the view stays on the table. Show it the robot pedestal or the empty floor and it paints a plausible lab that is not there.

Next.

  • A state-based teacher that solves the whole task in simulation, distilled into a visuomotor student on repainted frames.
  • Pick the renderer by what it does to learning, not by how it looks: train clones of one policy briefly on the same states repainted by each candidate and keep the one whose action error on real images falls fastest.
  • Repeat the training comparison at 512 to 1024 demonstrations with checkpoint selection in augsim.
  • Model the arm's settling offset in the simulator.

F. Comparison with RoboRender

RoboRender (Huang, Ai, Li Fei-Fei, Jiajun Wu and others, October 2026) asks whether a video model can make simulated data good enough to train real policies at scale, and answers yes for visually driven tasks. This project asks how little real data is enough, whether a repainted sim can evaluate a policy, and what remains when the task is contact-rich.
